## Friction points

_The hardest part was not the product concept but making the numbers and the regulation hold together.

The first friction was the CCD2 argument. My early "why now" said that launching before November 2026 would exempt the card from CCD2. That was wrong — there is no exemption, and a friends-and-family launch would fall after the deadline anyway. Correcting it actually strengthened the case: CCD2 does not exempt a card, but it makes checkout BNPL more expensive per purchase, because every purchase needs a full affordability assessment, while a card does that assessment once. The lesson was that a weak argument presented confidently is more dangerous than an honest assumption clearly labelled.

The second was metric consistency. My pilot thresholds required 70% activation while targeting 75% usage among approved users — which is impossible, since usage cannot exceed activation. It only became visible when I lined up all the files next to each other. Each module looked correct on its own; the contradiction lived between them.

The third was economics. EU interchange on consumer cards is capped at 0.30%, which on the brief's year-one volume is only around €375k. That forced me to accept that this is a lending product with a payment rail, not a payments product — and that changes what the MVP has to prove._

## Key learnings

_The promise is only as good as the mechanism behind it. "Keep it first, pay later" sounds simple, but on a card the merchant is paid immediately. What makes the promise true is that refunds net off the balance before anything is due. My day-to-day work on Riverty's first card programme showed me that card refunds arrive linked to the original purchase rather than to a bill, so this netting has to be designed deliberately. That moved refund netting from "next" to "now" in my roadmap.
Guardrails can be undermined by your own features. Partner-merchant rewards looked like a quick win for the merchant-first strategy. But rewarding card use at partner merchants would have encouraged exactly the cannibalisation my guardrail was meant to catch.
The real competitor is indifference. Lena is not frustrated with Riverty; she simply doesn't think of it. That changes the problem from "remove friction" to "give her a reason".
Brand cannot be the lever. Against Klarna's awareness in Germany, Riverty's 6% preference means the card has to win on timing and control, not on recognition._

_____

## Aha! moment

_The aha moment was realising that CCD2 is not a compliance obstacle for this card — it is the strategic argument for it. I had been treating regulation as the thing that slows launches down, because that is how it shows up in daily delivery work. But for this proposition, the same rule that adds cost to every BNPL checkout decision is what makes a single upfront assessment, followed by a card used everywhere, structurally more efficient. Once I saw that, the "why now" stopped being a list of reasons and became one coherent story: CCD2 taxes every checkout credit decision, and a card pays that tax once._
